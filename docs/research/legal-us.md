# Topic: legal-us

## Summary

Status as of 2026-09-01. (1) Securities delivery: fractional shares can only be delivered into a brokerage account opened at an SEC-registered broker-dealer/FINRA member (Stash: Stash Capital LLC introducing + Apex Clearing; Bits of Stock/Grifin/Bumped: Apex; Acorns: Acorns Securities). Every live program requires the recipient to open and KYC a brokerage account (CIP: name, DOB, address, TIN; effectively 18+) and the sponsor/partner funds the shares; none is structured as a chance-based sweepstakes except Robinhood-style referral stock, which uses disclosed odds (99% $5 / 0.9% $10 / 0.1% $200, $1,500/yr cap, 3-day sell lock, 30-day withdrawal lock). The 1999 SEC "free stock" orders hold that stock given for registering, supplying data or referring others is a "sale for value" under Securities Act 2(a)(3) — an issuer cannot give away its own securities for gameplay; giving away third-party listed stock bought on the open market through a broker avoids Section 5 but triggers FINRA 2210 communications rules, the 2021 SEC DEP concern about "games, streaks, prizes... free stock", the Massachusetts gamification precedent ($7.5M, 2024), and FINRA Rule 2040/Exchange Act 15(a) if the broker pays the game operator per account (finder/unregistered-broker risk). (2) Gambling: prize + chance + consideration; consideration can be non-monetary ("considerable time or effort"), so randomized rewards for gameplay are the highest-risk design; deterministic, fixed-ratio rewards remove "chance" and are the only form Google Play permits for game loyalty programs. NY/FL require registration and bonding above $5,000 (NY 30 days, FL 7 days); RI $500 for retail-only. Eleven-plus states banned dual-currency sweepstakes casinos in 2025-26 (CA AB 831 effective 1/1/2026 with vendor liability), and Washington's "thing of value" cases (Big Fish $155M, DoubleDown $415M) show even non-cashable virtual chips can be gambling. GPT apps (Mistplay, Mode) are 18+, call points "promotional... no cash value", and reserve cancellation rights. (3) Token: the SEC-CFTC joint interpretive release (Rel. 33-11412, effective 3/23/2026) classifies crypto assets into digital commodities, digital collectibles (expressly including "in-game items"), digital tools (memberships, tickets, credentials), stablecoins and digital securities; the first three are not securities but can be sold under an investment contract that ends when purchasers no longer rely on the issuer's essential managerial efforts. Its airdrop interpretation applies only to distributions for which recipients provide no consideration — tokens paid "in exchange for" gameplay or services are expressly outside it. Proposed Regulation Crypto Assets (Rel. 33-11434, FR 8/21/2026, comments due 10/20/2026) adds a $5M/4-yr startup exemption, a $75M/12-mo fundraising exemption and a Subpart D safe harbor. A token redeemable for stock at a fixed rate is a security (custodial receipt) or, if it only tracks price, a security-based swap that cannot be sold to retail non-ECPs (SEC staff 1/28/2026). CLARITY passed the House 7/17/2025, cleared Senate Banking 5/14/2026, is on the Senate calendar with a cloture vote reported for 9/15/2026 and needs 60 votes; GENIUS is law (7/18/2025; effective no later than 1/18/2027; no yield to holders). (4) Money transmission: an "administrator" that issues and redeems convertible value is a money transmitter; SEC/CFTC-regulated persons are exempt; NY BitLicense excludes units usable only inside a game with no outside market and not convertible to fiat/crypto, and rewards-program units. A closed-loop, non-convertible token avoids MSB status; an on-chain transferable token convertible to stock or cash does not. (5) FTC: Endorsement Guides (2023), Consumer Reviews Rule (16 CFR 465, eff. 10/21/2024; incentivized reviews conditioned on sentiment banned), pending Earnings Claim Rule and 2026 earnings-claim actions; CFPB is "monitoring" game currencies. (6) App stores: Apple bars crypto apps from paying currency for downloading apps or social posting (3.1.5(v)), requires loot-box odds, requires sweepstakes be developer-sponsored with in-app rules, and permits incentives for in-app actions; Google requires fixed-ratio, non-chance loyalty rewards in games, licensed crypto exchanges (FinCEN MSB + state MT), and bans paying for a chance at NFTs. (7) Tax: prizes reportable on 1099-MISC box 3 at FMV when >= $2,000/yr (OBBBA, payments after 12/31/2025); rewards for services go on 1099-NEC; COPPA amendments in force with 4/22/2026 compliance date.

## Findings (35)

1. **[high]** SEC In re Joe Loofbourrow (Securities Act Rel. 33-7700 / Exchange Act Rel. 34-41631, July 21, 1999): 'free' stock given to people who filled out a registration form (name, address, email), answered questions and referred others was a 'sale' under Section 5 because 'the lack of monetary consideration for the free shares does not mean that there was not a sale'; the issuer received value (site traffic, prospective investors, creation of a market). One of four July 1999 free-stock cases.
   - Source: https://www.sec.gov/enforcement-litigation/administrative-proceedings/34-41631 (1999-07-21)
   - Relevance: An issuer cannot give away its own stock (or any security, including a token that is a security) as a gameplay/referral reward without registration or an exemption; giving away third-party listed stock purchased on the open market through a broker is the only model in use.

6. **[high]** FINRA Rule 2040(a): no member may pay 'any compensation, fees, concessions, discounts, commissions or other allowances' to a person not registered as a broker-dealer who 'by reason of receipt of any such payments and the activities related thereto, is required to be so registered' under Exchange Act 15(a); members must 'reasonably support' a determination that the recipient need not register (SEC releases, no-action letters, legal opinion). Effective Aug 24, 2015.
   - Source: https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/2040 (2015-08-24)
   - Relevance: If a partner broker pays the game operator per funded account or per referral, the game operator risks being an unregistered broker/finder; structure compensation as flat marketing fees not tied to transactions or account values, or register.

7. **[high]** FINRA Regulatory Notice 26-05 (Feb 27, 2026): amendments effective March 30, 2026 raise the Rule 3220 gift limit from $100 to $300 per person per year, with conforming $300 limits in the non-cash compensation rules 2310, 2320, 2341 and 5110; those rules govern gifts to employees of others and non-cash compensation tied to sales of DPPs, variable contracts, investment company securities and underwritings, plus exemptive relief for good cause.

12. **[medium]** Sweepstakes law basics: an illegal lottery requires prize, chance and consideration; consideration includes not only purchase or entry fees but non-monetary forms such as 'an entrant's expenditure of considerable time or effort'; a prize awarded on skill is a 'contest'; a purchase generally cannot be required as a condition of entry; NY and FL require registration and 'a bond in an amount equal to the total value of the offered prizes' when total prizes exceed $5,000 (RI: $500 for retail promotions). AMOE must be 'substantially equivalent... in terms of effort, time, and accessibility' with equal odds (Snipp, Feb 12, 2026).
   - Source: https://www.olshanlaw.com/sweepstakes-law-basics (2026-09-01 (accessed; undated page))
   - Relevance: Because gameplay time can be consideration, any chance-based reward in an idle game risks lottery classification in strict states; deterministic (skill/effort-only) rewards remove the 'chance' element. AMOE detail: https://www.snipp.com/blog/quick-guide-to-sweepstakes-laws-and-rules.

19. **[high]** SEC-CFTC 'Application of the Federal Securities Laws to Certain Types of Crypto Assets' (Rel. 33-11412/34-105020, adopted March 17, 2026, FR March 23, 2026, effective March 23, 2026) classifies crypto assets into five categories: digital commodities, digital collectibles, digital tools, stablecoins, digital securities. 'Digital commodities, digital collectibles, and digital tools... are not themselves securities'; a digital collectible 'may represent or convey rights to artwork, music, videos, trading cards, in-game items'; a digital tool 'performs a practical function, such as a membership, ticket, credential, title instrument, or identity badge' and has no 'passive yield or... rights to future income'. A non-security asset 'can be offered and sold subject to an investment contract' when the issuer induces investment with promises of 'essential managerial efforts', and the contract ends when purchasers can no longer reasonably expect those efforts. CFTC guidance: it will administer the CEA consistent with the interpretation and 'certain non-security crypto assets could meet the definition of commodity'.
   - Source: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/03/23/2026-05635/application-of-the-federal-securities-laws-to-certain-types-of-crypto-assets-and-certain (2026-03-23)
   - Relevance: Primary federal taxonomy: an in-game utility token with no yield/profit rights fits 'digital tool'/'digital collectible' and is not itself a security, but marketing it with promises of value appreciation from the developer's efforts creates an investment contract; CFTC may treat it as a commodity.

20. **[high]** Same release, Section VII (airdrops): an airdrop of a non-security crypto asset is not an investment contract because 'the first element of the Howey test—requiring an investment of money—is not met' — but only for 'recipients who do not provide the issuer with money, goods, services, or other consideration in exchange'; the interpretation 'does not pertain to any airdrops... where the recipient provides the issuer with money, goods, services, or other consideration in exchange for the airdropped non-security crypto asset, such as where the recipient performs a service in exchange'. Section 2(a)(3) 'sale' concepts 'do not apply to airdrops of non-security crypto assets that are not subject to an investment contract'.
   - Source: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/03/23/2026-05635/application-of-the-federal-securities-laws-to-certain-types-of-crypto-assets-and-certain (2026-03-23)
   - Relevance: Game tokens distributed 'in exchange for' gameplay, attention, referrals or data fall outside the SEC's airdrop comfort; the token's non-security status must instead rest on it being a digital tool/collectible with no investment-contract promises.

25. **[high]** CLARITY text as referred to the Senate (H.R. 3633 RFS): 'digital commodity' means 'a digital asset that is intrinsically linked to a blockchain system, and the value of which is derived from or is reasonably expected to be derived from the use of the blockchain system' (including assets 'used to access the activities or services of the blockchain system'); 'mature blockchain system' = 'not controlled by any person or group of persons under common control'; 'investment contract asset' = a digital commodity transferable person-to-person 'without necessary reliance on an intermediary' sold under an investment contract; 'end user distribution' = a distribution that 'does not involve an exchange of more than a nominal value of cash, property, or other assets' and is 'distributed in a broad and equitable manner... including, as incentive-based rewards... to users'; the new Securities Act 4(a)(8) exemption in this text caps proceeds at 'not more than $50,000,000' per 12 months (CPI-adjusted) — many summaries cite $75 million from other versions.
   - Source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr3633rfs/html/BILLS-119hr3633rfs.htm (2025-07 (House-passed text))
   - Relevance: If enacted, free/incentive token distributions to game users could qualify as 'end user distributions' outside the investment-contract regime; design token rewards to be nominal-consideration, broadly available, and tied to network use.
